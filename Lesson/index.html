Spherical Coordinates

Spherical coordinates, typically denoted as (r, θ, ϕ), are useful for describing positions on a sphere or spheroid. Spherical coordinates are a powerful three-dimensional coordinate system essential in physics for simplifying problems involving points, spheres, or central forces such as gravity, electrostatics, and quantum mechanics. To better explain physics concepts, this guide will frequently employ spherical coordinates, so it is useful to become familiar with them.

Unlike Cartesian coordinates (x, y, z), which use three linear distances to locate a point, spherical coordinates define a position using one radial distance r (or ρ) from the origin and two angles: θ and ϕ, which measure inclination from the z-axis and rotation around the xy-plane. While Cartesian coordinates are ideal for linear or box-shaped objects, they make calculations involving circles or spheres very complex. In contrast, a sphere centered at the origin is naturally described in spherical coordinates, reducing complicated three-dimensional problems into manageable and organized calculations.

In terms of Cartesian coordinates:

x = r cos θ sin ϕ,   y = r sin θ sin ϕ,   z = r cos ϕ
02

Brahe’s Astronomical Data

Tycho Brahe originally collected astronomical data of Mars’ position in the sky using the Alt-Azimuth Coordinate System, the Ecliptic Coordinate System, and the Equatorial Coordinate System. Later, Johannes Kepler used Brahe’s data to develop his three laws of planetary motion.

2.1 Horizontal (Alt-Azimuth) Coordinate System

The Alt-Azimuth Coordinate System, also known as the Horizontal Coordinate System, is a celestial coordinate system that uses the observer’s local horizon as the fundamental reference plane. It is one of the simplest and most intuitive systems for locating objects in the sky because it describes positions relative to what an observer directly sees from a specific location on Earth. This system was useful for making direct observations from a specific location on Earth because it described objects relative to the local horizon and allowed Brahe to measure where stars and planets appeared in the sky at a given time.

  • Altitude (a): 0°at the horizon, 90°at the zenith
  • Azimuth (A): Compass direction — 0°North, 90°East, etc.

2.2 Ecliptic Coordinate System

The Ecliptic Coordinate System is centered around Earth’s orbital plane, known as the ecliptic, around the Sun. It measures two angles: ecliptic longitude and ecliptic latitude.

Ecliptic Longitude (λ) measures the angular distance of a celestial object along the ecliptic plane. It is measured eastward from the vernal equinox, which is the point where the Sun crosses the celestial equator moving northward during the March equinox. Ecliptic longitude ranges from 0°to 360°.

Ecliptic Latitude (β) measures the angular distance of an object above or below the ecliptic plane. Objects located directly on the ecliptic have a latitude of 0°, while objects north of the plane have positive values and objects south of the plane have negative values. Since planets move close to Earth’s orbital plane around the Sun, this system made it easier for Brahe to study planetary motion and compare the changing positions of planets.

  • Ecliptic Longitude (λ): Angle from the vernal equinox
  • Ecliptic Latitude (β): Angle above or below the ecliptic

2.3 Equatorial Coordinate System

The Equatorial Coordinate System is centered around the celestial equator, which is tilted approximately 23.44°relative to the ecliptic plane. This system is independent of the observer’s location and remains constant throughout both night and day.

  • Declination (δ): +90°at the north celestial pole, 0°at the celestial equator, and −90°at the south celestial pole
  • Right Ascension (α): Measured in hours (24 h = 360°), starting from the vernal equinox
03

Kepler’s Laws

Johannes Kepler initially believed that planetary orbits were circular, as had been assumed for centuries. For many of the planets, such as Venus and Jupiter, circular orbit models appeared to work fairly well. However, Mars was an exception due to its higher eccentricity; when Kepler analyzed the motion of Mars using Brahe’s observational data, he found that circular models could not accurately match Brahe’s precise measurements.

Through mathematical analysis and extensive trial and error of different orbits including offset circles, asymmetric ovals, and symmetric ovals, Kepler eventually determined that planets move in elliptical orbits with the Sun located at one focus. This discovery led to the development of Kepler’s First Law of Planetary Motion.

3.1 Why Was Mars Unique?

For many planets, including Venus and Jupiter, circular orbit models appeared to match observations reasonably well. Because the deviations were small, astronomers for centuries believed that planets moved in perfect circles around Earth or the Sun. Mars, however, behaved differently. Its orbit has a noticeably higher eccentricity, meaning its path is more stretched out rather than nearly circular.

Using Tycho Brahe’s highly accurate astronomical measurements, Kepler compared the predicted positions of Mars from circular orbit models to Mars’s actual observed positions in the sky. When Kepler analyzed Tycho Brahe’s extremely precise observations of Mars, he discovered that the planet’s predicted position from a circular model consistently disagreed with the actual observed position. Unlike with other planets, the discrepancy for Mars was too large to ignore.

Kepler found a discrepancy of approximately 8 arcminutes between the theoretical predictions and Brahe’s data. Although 8 arcminutes is only about one-eighth of the diameter of the full Moon, Kepler considered the error too significant to dismiss because Brahe’s instruments were precise enough that such a difference could not simply be blamed on measurement inaccuracies. Rather than adjusting the data to fit the accepted theory, Kepler trusted the observations and concluded that the model itself was flawed. This small discrepancy ultimately became one of the most important pieces of evidence in astronomy, as it led Kepler to abandon circular orbits and develop the theory of elliptical planetary motion.

Introduction

You probably know what a force is. In a rough sense, an object attracts another. Simple enough. These ”forces” follow a set of rules that were deemed Newton’s Laws through Newton’s own observation and were shown to be correct in most cases, as for the exceptions, you’ll see them in a higher-level physics class.

Definition 1.1. Newton’s Laws are summarized as follows:

  • Newton’s First Law: An object in rest stays at rest, and an object in motion stays in motion with the same speed and in the same direction unless acted upon by an unbalanced external force.
  • Newton’s Second Law: The acceleration of an object is directly proportional to the net force acting on it and inversely proportional to its mass.
  • Newton’s Third Law: For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction. Forces always occur in pairs.

This is all well and good. An apple falls onto Newton’s head with an acceleration to the force acting on it (gravity) and hits Newton (quite hard) and Newton feels a reaction force (with a bump on his head). It is natural then to wonder whether these laws extend into higher dimensions of size.

For massive objects, do these laws still hold? We may contend these are true for a large rock, a house, and an Eiffel tower. But, say, does it hold for a planet? For such a huge object, does it follow a certain path in motion or can it stop? What happens with a collision or around another object?

These questions start with the formulation of a gravitational force. A force that is defined to be the law of gravity between two objects. This was formulated by Newton as follows.

Definition 1.2. The gravitational force between two objects m1 and m2 is equal to

F = Gm₁m₂/r²

where r is the distance between m1 and m2.

At first glance, this formula is calculational, but there are other interesting parts. For instance, what exactly is G?

Let us first understand it in terms of units. Rearranging to get G, we have:

G = Fr²/(m₁m₂)

The units of force [F] is Newtons N or kg·m/s². Hence, the units of G are

[G] = [F] · [r]² / ([m₁] · [m₂]) = (kg · m/s²) · (m²/kg²) = m³/(kg · s²)

This G is known as the gravitational constant: G = 6.67 · 10⁻¹¹ Nm²kg² to be precise. Essentially, this the scale of the gravitational attraction between two objects.

Now imagine that we exist as an object with a large shell. A shell can be thought of as a cover that has mass on the outside edge. Since we exist without another mass, we may think there is no gravitational force. This is both right and wrong. These theorems attributed to Newton can shed some light on this matter.

1.3

Theorem 1.3

The gravitational acceleration within a hollow, uniformly dense spherical shell is 0 for r < R where R represents the radius of the shell. However, the gravitational acceleration outside the shell is as if all the mass of the shell were concentrated at its center for r ≥R.

A pictorial description is below:

While the formal derivation of these theorems requires some difficult techniques, we can intuitively understand this theorem.

When r > R, effectively a mass m is outside of the sphere. Thus if we consider the forces from the masses outside, they don’t cancel out. However, the vertical components do cancel because they are opposite to each other. Since they don’t cancel, we can reason that it should be nonzero. To prove the exact relationship lies a bit more effort, but we can think of the situation as adding a bunch of opposite vectors (two points whose chord is perpendicular to the line connecting point P to C) whose resultant (the addition of the vectors) lies on PC.

For the case of r < R, the point mass P may be closer to one side than the other. If it is in the ”perfect” center of the circle, then clearly the force vectors cancel out, so the net force is 0. Hence, in the case that it is closer to one side than the other, the side closer provides a larger force (since the mass corresponding to the arc is larger) than that of the farther side. However, the side farther from P has more distance from P than the side closer to P. These effects of strength over length adequately balance each other out to get 0.

Kepler’s Laws

While this formulation of the force of gravity between objects does answer some questions that are mentioned, we still don’t have an answer for the path of these planetary objects. Through Newton’s Laws, Johannes Kepler explained what exactly happens to these masses.

Lemma 2.1

Kepler’s Laws for a general orbit are

  • The trajectories of planets are sections, with a focus at the Sun. Bound orbits are ellipses, circles if the eccentricity is 1.
  • The Trajectories sweep out equal areas in equal times.
  • When the orbit is bound, the period T and semimajor axis a obey T² ∝ a³.

A pictorial view of these Laws is below:

While these Laws are complex, we can get an intuitive understanding for each. The full derivation is complex, and you will see them in a higher level physics class. However, we can still get an appreciation of them using simple techniques.

Kepler’s 1st Law may be unmotivated for those of you who have not worked with ellipses before, so take a leap of faith that it holds.

Kepler’s 2nd Law can be explained intuitively like so: take a part of the area that the mass goes over a period of time. This A/t is equal to ½r · v because this is the only dimensionally correct interpretation (or think of it as a ”right” triangle) and the factor of ½ is there as if it were not, it would be a parallelogram, which is not necessarily correct.

The ΔA corresponds to a change in time. We can ”assume” that sin θ tends to 1, so this is effectively saying that the change in area is equal to ½rv. Recall that the angular momentum is L = m · v · r, so v · r = L/m, which implies

ΔA = L/(2m)

but the angular momentum is constant throughout an object’s orbit, so ΔA is constant. Do note that this is an intuitive argument, and there are some details to be ironed out to make it rigorous. But, this will work for us.

Finally, let’s consider Kepler’s Third Law in that T² ∝ a³. We can imagine a circle (as sort of a cheat code), with a = R. Imagine a point mass is in orbit R (directly above the planet). Let the velocity of the object in orbit be v. Then, the time to complete an orbit is

T = 2πr/v

We can also directly find v through the formula connecting the centripetal force to the gravitational force:

mv²/r = GMm/r²   ⇒   v² = GM/r

Hence

T² = 4π²r²/v² = 4π²r² · r/(GM) ∝ r³

Problems

Take a partner and pick the 2 to 3 problems in this list that look the most interesting to do.

PROBLEM 3.1

Alex is on a planet covered with loose rock on its surface. Alex is interested in creating a space station in a geostationary orbit around this planet. Is it possible to Alex to do so, regardless of the planet’s rotation speed?

Suggested Solution

Model-generated explanation · not an official answer key

Assume an isolated spherical planet of mass M, radius R, and nonzero angular speed ω. A stationary satellite must follow a circular equatorial orbit in the same direction as the rotation, with the same period.

GM/r² = ω²r   ⇒   rsync = (GM/ω²)1/3

An orbit above the surface requires rsync > R, or ω²R³ < GM. If rotation is at the surface orbital limit, the synchronous radius is R; if it is faster, the formal radius is inside the planet.

Loose equatorial rocks would already lose contact when centrifugal acceleration reaches surface gravity. Thus a stable planet retaining loose surface rocks is below that limit in this ideal model. A nonrotating planet has no finite synchronous orbit, and extremely slow rotation may place a real satellite beyond the region where the planet can retain it against external perturbations. So “regardless of rotation speed” is too strong.

PROBLEM 3.2

Alex has rephrased Kepler’s Laws as follows

  • (a) the orbits of planets are elliptical with one focus at the sun.
  • (b) a line connecting the sun and a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times.
  • (c) the square of the period of a planet’s orbit is proportional to the cube of its semimajor axis.

Alex knows that these laws hold when the force of gravity is proportional to 1/r². However, he wonders whether any of these would hold if the force of the gravity is proportional to 1/r³. Can you help Alex?

Suggested Solution

Model-generated explanation · not an official answer key

Only (b) remains generally true. An inverse-cube force is still radial, so its torque is zero and angular momentum is conserved.

(a) does not hold in general: the inverse-square law is essential to the usual focus-centered ellipses. Special circular solutions can exist, but general inverse-cube trajectories are not Kepler ellipses.

(c) fails even for those circular solutions. Write the force magnitude as k/r³, with k fixed:

mv²/r = k/r³   ⇒   v² = k/(mr²)
T² = 4π²mr⁴/k ∝ r⁴

Thus the circular period scaling is r⁴ rather than r³. This is a comparison of exact circular solutions, not a claim of stable Kepler-like elliptical orbits.

PROBLEM 3.3

Alex is in a spaceship orbiting a planet. Alex is prone to falling asleep on the wheel since there are no space cops to catch him. At those times, the ship must fire its thrusters to avoid falling into the atmosphere. Where should the thrusters point (the direction where exhaust is fired) to give the greatest increase in the maximum height of its orbit?

Suggested Solution

Model-generated explanation · not an official answer key

Under the usual intended assumption of a short burn at periapsis (or from an initially circular orbit), fire the exhaust backward, opposite the direction of travel. The reaction force accelerates the ship forward, in the prograde direction, raising apoapsis.

Δε = v · Δv + ½|Δv|²

For a fixed small impulse, energy gain is greatest when Δv is aligned with v. A periapsis burn is especially effective because speed is largest there.

Assumption matters: the prompt does not specify orbital phase, available impulse, or starting orbit. At a general point on an eccentric orbit, maximizing apoapsis is a distinct optimization involving both energy and angular momentum. The standard backward-exhaust answer assumes the periapsis/circular setting above; a single burn also does not guarantee safe atmospheric clearance on every later pass.

PROBLEM 3.4

The supermassive black hole at the center of the galaxy Axodus is called Gigantamax A∗with a mass of 8 × 10³⁶ kilograms. It takes 200 million years for the solar system to orbit once around the galaxy.

Under the assumption that the only object in this galaxy is Gigantamaz A∗, find the distance of the galazy from the center.

Previous explorers found the value to be 2 · 10²⁰ meters. Assuming this value is correct and given the approximation we made, does the value reached in the previous part make sense?

Suggested Solution

Model-generated explanation · not an official answer key

Assume a circular orbit; otherwise the calculation gives a semimajor axis rather than an instantaneous distance. Using 365.25 days per year:

T = 2 × 10⁸ years ≈ 6.31152 × 10¹⁵ s
r = [GMT²/(4π²)]1/3 ≈ 8.14 × 10¹⁸ m

This is about 24.6 times smaller than 2 × 10²⁰ m. The discrepancy is consistent with having omitted most of the gravitating material. At the stated measured radius and period, a spherical enclosed-mass estimate gives:

Menclosed = 4π²r³/(GT²) ≈ 1.19 × 10⁴¹ kg

That is about 1.49 × 10⁴ times the black-hole mass. The simplified model badly underestimates the mass needed for the reported orbit. A real galaxy’s mass distribution requires a more detailed model.

PROBLEM 3.5

Adapted from F=MA A spherical mist of miniscule particles in space has a uniform density p0 and a radius r0. The gravitational acceleration of free fall at the surface of the mist due to the mass of the mist is g0. A process occurs that causes the mist to suddenly grow to a radius 2R0, while maintaining a uniform density, which may or may not be equal to the original density. What is the new gravitational acceleration of free fall at a point r0 away from the center of the mist?

Don’t be scared by the process, look at gravity.

Suggested Solution

Model-generated explanation · not an official answer key

The shell theorem says that only the mass inside radius r₀ contributes at that point. For final density ρ₁:

gnew = (4πG/3)ρ₁r₀ = (ρ₁/ρ₀)g₀

If total mass is conserved, doubling the radius multiplies the volume by 8, so ρ₁ = ρ₀/8 and gnew = g₀/8.

If density stayed fixed instead, the answer would be g₀, but that would require adding mass. Without specifying mass conservation or the final density, the prompt does not determine a unique numerical fraction.

PROBLEM 3.6

The ∆v in spaceflight is defined as the magnitude of the change in velocity needed during a particular maneuver in space. What is the ∆v needed to escape Planet X’s gravitational pull from low-planet orbit? Let Planet X have mass m and radius r.

Suggested Solution

Model-generated explanation · not an official answer key

Assume an initially circular orbit with negligible altitude, no atmosphere, and one instantaneous prograde burn. The existing orbital speed counts toward escape:

vcirc = √(Gm/r),   vesc = √(2Gm/r)
Δv = vesc − vcirc = (√2 − 1)√(Gm/r)

This is the minimum ideal impulse for zero speed at infinity. For an orbit at altitude h, replace r with r + h. It is not the launch Δv from the surface.

PROBLEM 3.7

Imagine that at a given point in time, the Plant X, Plant Y, and Planet Z lie on the same line (collinear). How many days will it be before they are all collinear again? Let Planet X have period 100 days, planet Y have period 200 days, and Planet Z have period 400 days.

Suggested Solution

Model-generated explanation · not an official answer key

Under the standard intended model—uniform circular coplanar orbits in the same direction, initially aligned along a line through their common central body—both relative angles must return to multiples of 180°.

(1/100 − 1/200)t = t/200 ∈ ½ℤ
(1/200 − 1/400)t = t/400 ∈ ½ℤ

The first condition requires t to be a multiple of 100 days; the second requires a multiple of 200 days. The earliest positive common value is 200 days. X and Y return to their original directions while Z is on the opposite side.

Scope: this is alignment along a line through the central body. Literal collinearity of only the three planets on an arbitrary line can occur at other times. Periods alone, without geometry and initial positions, do not determine that more general answer.

PROBLEM 3.8

Imagine a drill is dug down to the center of the a Planet X. A mass m is placed a depth d into the drill at a distance r = rx −d from the center of the Earth where rx is the radius of planet X. What is the weight of the object at this point? Solve in terms of the mass of Planet X, radius of Planet X, mass of object, and distance of the mass into the drill.

Suggested Solution

Model-generated explanation · not an official answer key

Assume uniform planetary density and negligible mass removed by the drill. The enclosed mass at radius r is M(r/R)³. Outer spherical layers exert no net force there.

W = GmM(r/R)³/r² = GMm(R − d)/R³
W = (GMm/R²)(1 − d/R)

The gravitational force points toward the center, decreases linearly with depth, and vanishes at the center. The range is 0 ≤ d ≤ R.

Without uniform density, use W = GmMenclosed(R − d)/(R − d)². The total mass and radius alone are insufficient. This is gravitational weight; apparent weight also depends on support and rotation.

PROBLEM 3.9

On Planet Robo of mass M and radius R, robots have dug out a spherical area of radius R/2 as shown. Originally, Planet Robo had mass M. Find the acceleration due to gravity at the top of the cavern and the acceleration at the bottom.

Suggested Solution

Model-generated explanation · not an official answer key

Assume the original planet has uniform density. Let vector a point from the planet’s center to the cavern’s center. Model the excavation as the original sphere minus a smaller sphere of the same density.

gfull(x) = −(GM/R³)x
gremoved(x) = −(GM/R³)(x − a)
gcavity = gfull − gremoved = −(GM/R³)a

The field is uniform throughout the cavity, so its value at the top and bottom is the same: magnitude GM|a|/R³, directed opposite to the center-offset vector.

If the intended cavern is internally tangent to the surface, then |a| = R/2, and both accelerations have magnitude GM/(2R²). A concentric cavern instead has a = 0 and zero field inside. The missing diagram prevents choosing one geometry as the official answer.

04

Reduced Mass

Having gone through the previous problems, you may ask the following question: ”Given several planets, how exactly can we configure a coordinate system that handles each planet simplistically?” Obviously we can plot a dot as the origin, but then considering each planet from that origin seems messy.

This question will be answered in the following discussion. But let us first consider the following example.

Example 4.1

Consider two planets, both of mass m, say A and B. Alex holds A in place (yes he’s that strong), and B performs a circular orbit of radius r and period t. Then, Alex lets go of both planets, and they perform circular orbits simultaneously of radius r/2 about their center of mass. What is the new period of this motion?

Let us consider each situation alone. In the former, planet A acts as the origin of our system, and planet B merely orbits planet A. Let Planet B have velocity v, then we can write the following through a centripetal acceleration calculation:

mv²/r = Gm²/r²   ⇒   v = √(Gm/r)

In the latter situation, the only thing that changes is that the center of mass differs, resulting in a new radius of r/2. The planets still interact solely through gravitational forces, which is separated by a distance of 2 · r/2 or r.Therefore:

mv²/(r/2) = Gm²/r²   ⇒   v = (1/√2)√(Gm/r)

In this case the velocity is smaller by a factor of 1/√2. The motion is also a factor of 2 smaller due to the radius. Hence the period is t/√2.

This is all well and good, but we may wonder if there exists a setup that makes things easier. The first situation was fine because planet A was the origin, so it essentially wasn’t there. The second one was also not too much trouble, but could there be a better way?

The answer is yes. The following fact may seem unintuitive, but have trust that it holds.

Lemma 4.2

Consider two objects of mass m1 and m2 with positions r1 and r2. Their relative position is r2 −r1 under the appropriate gravitational forces of the system. For the purposes of computationally computing characteristics of this system, velocities, forces, radii, etc, we may replace this system with a single mass µ in the same system. µ satisfies the equation

1/μ = 1/m₁ + 1/m₂

This new system with the reduced mass μ is isomorphic to the other coordinate system.

The word ”isomorphic” may seem unwieldy, but it just states that nothing changes from the previous system to this one. All the forces, distances, etc when calculated properly work out to be the same.

Now, let us see how to apply this lemma in the same example. We can consider the relative position ra −rb between planets A and B. Notice that these planets orbit in a circle of radius r. Using the lemma, we can define µ to be

μ = 1/(1/m + 1/m) = m/2

which acts in a circular orbit of radius r as the problem describes.

As our ”reduced mass” system is the ”same” as the original system, the forces between masses stays the same, so the centripetal force is Gm²/r². Therefore, the reduced mass μ fulfills the equation:

μv²/r = Gm²/r²   ⇒   v = √(2Gm/r)

Notice that it is r and not r/2 because this reduced mass has an orbit of a circle of radius r. Since the speed is bigger by a factor of √2, but the length of the orbit is the same, the period becomes t/√2.

PROBLEM 4.3

This is a hard problem, but very fun.

Alex has two objects that act under a net force that is attractive. No torques are generated, so effectively these two objects interact through a central force. If one of the objects is held still (again Alex is strong) and the other is released, the period of collision is T. If instead Alex holds the other object, the period of collision is T ′. If instead both objects were released from rest, how long does is the period of collision?

Suggested Solution

Model-generated explanation · not an official answer key

Assume the same initial separation, the same position-dependent mutual force in all three experiments, and the same collision separation. For a fixed force law F(r), the fall-time integral scales as the square root of the inertial mass governing the separation.

If the masses are m₁ and m₂, then T = C√m₂ and T′ = C√m₁, where C is the same force-and-geometry factor. When both move, the separation is governed by μ = m₁m₂/(m₁ + m₂), so τ = C√μ.

1/τ² = 1/T² + 1/T′²
τ = TT′ / √(T² + T′²)

For equal masses, T = T′ and τ = T/√2. The result does not require an inverse-square force, but it does require the common position-dependent force and release conditions specified above.
