Vectors & Ellipses

Abir Mehta

The 2nd and 3rd Law derivations build on a few math ideas: vectors, the cross product, the dot product, and the geometry of an ellipse. None of it is particularly challenging, but dropping it in the middle of a physics derivation just gets in the way. So everything sits here on its own page. Read the tidbits you need, then jump back to whichever law you were working on.

Note that one thing we are not doing on this page is proving that orbits are ellipses in the first place. That’s Kepler’s 1st Law, and it’s a separate derivation. Here we just need to know what an ellipse is so the rest makes sense.

Vectors

A vector is just a quantity with both a magnitude and a direction. Velocity is a vector (50 km/h, heading north); so is force, and so is position. We draw one as an arrow: the length is the magnitude, and the way it points is the direction. We write vectors with a little arrow on top, like $\vec{A}$, and the plain symbol $A$ (no arrow) means just the magnitude.

A unit vector is a vector whose length is exactly $1$. All it tells you is direction. We put a hat on it instead of an arrow, like $\hat{r}$. Unit vectors are handy because any vector can be split into “how long” times “which way”: for example, $\vec{r} = r\hat{r}$ means “a distance $r$ in the $\hat{r}$ direction.” This is the notation we’ll work with in the 2nd Law setup.

The Vector Cross Product

The vector cross product seems like a tricky concept, but understanding its physical representation is more fundamental to your understanding of it.

Suppose, we have two vectors $\vec{A}$ and $\vec{B}$. Their cross product $\vec{A} \times \vec{B}$ is another vector $\vec{C}$. $\vec{C}$ has magnitude $|\vec{A}||\vec{B}|\sin \theta_{AB}$ where $\theta_{AB}$ is the angle between vector $\vec{A}$ and $\vec{B}$. More importantly, however, $\vec{C}$ is mutually perpendicular (orthogonal) to both $\vec{A}$ and $\vec{B}$. Not sure what that looks like? Check out the figure below!

Note that if $\vec{A}$ and $\vec{B}$ are parallel or anti-parallel, then $\theta_{AB} = 0$ or $\pi$, so $|\vec{C}| = |\vec{A}||\vec{B}|\sin \theta_{AB} = 0$. In other words, the cross product of two aligned vectors is always zero.

That’s basically all you need to know! The rest of the information will be specific to the setup of the problem, and accessible from your AP Physics knowledge.

The Dot Product

The dot product is the other way to multiply two vectors. Just like with the cross product, understanding what it represents matters more than the formula itself.

Suppose, we have two vectors $\vec{A}$ and $\vec{B}$. Their dot product $\vec{A} \cdot \vec{B}$ is a scalar—just a number, not a vector. It is equal to $|\vec{A}||\vec{B}|\cos \theta_{AB}$ where $\theta_{AB}$ is the angle between vector $\vec{A}$ and $\vec{B}$. Notice that when $\vec{A}$ and $\vec{B}$ point in the same direction, $\theta_{AB} = 0$, so $\cos \theta_{AB} = 1$ and the dot product is at its largest. When they are perpendicular, $\theta_{AB} = 90^\circ$, so $\cos \theta_{AB} = 0$ and the dot product is zero. Not sure what that looks like? Check out the figure below!

Note that this is the opposite of the cross product: the dot product is zero when the two vectors are perpendicular, while the cross product is zero when they are parallel.

Ellipses

You’ve worked with circles forever, but ellipses probably haven’t gotten as much attention—and Kepler’s laws are full of them so let’s try to get familiar with one. The good news is that an ellipse is basically a stretched-out circle. Here’s how you make one.

The two-thumbtack definition

Pick two fixed points and call them the foci (one focus, two foci). An ellipse is every point whose distances to those two foci add up to the same total. There’s a classic way to see this: take a loop of string, pin it down at two spots with thumbtacks, pull it tight with a pencil, and trace all the way around. Since the string length never changes, the two distances always add up to the same length, and you get a perfect ellipse. Check out the figure below!

Why should we care? It’s because of where the Sun sits. In Kepler’s 1st Law, a planet orbits in an ellipse with the Sun at one of the foci—not at the center. That’s exactly why a planet gets close to the Sun on one side of its orbit and swings far away on the other.

The vocabulary ($a$, $b$, $c$, and $e$)

Put the ellipse flat with its long direction along the $x$-axis and its center at the origin. There are four key lengths that we’ll discuss. The semi-major axis $a$ is the distance from the center out to the far end—the long way. The semi-minor axis $b$ is the distance from the center out to the side—the short way. The distance $c$ tells you how far each focus sits from the center. And the eccentricity $e = c/a$ is a single number for how squished the ellipse is: $e = 0$ gives a perfect circle, and as $e$ gets closer to $1$ the ellipse stretches out longer and skinnier.

Let’s check a couple of these

First up: the two distances always add to $2a$. To see it, slide your point all the way to the right end of the ellipse. From there, the distance to the far focus is the whole width minus a little, and the distance to the near focus is that little bit—add them and you just get the full width, which is $2a$. Since the total is fixed for every point, we get

$$r_1 + r_2 = 2a$$

for any point on the ellipse.

Next, the relationship between $a$, $b$, and $c$. Move your point up to the very top of the ellipse. By symmetry it’s the same distance from both foci, and since those distances add to $2a$, each one is exactly $a$. Now look at the triangle made by the center, one focus, and that top point: its legs are $b$ and $c$, and its hypotenuse is $a$. Using Pythagorean theorem, gives us

$$a^2 = b^2 + c^2.$$

Where the equation comes from

If you want the actual equation of the ellipse, put the foci at $(-c, 0)$ and $(c, 0)$ and write the definition out for a point $(x, y)$:

$$\sqrt{(x+c)^2 + y^2} + \sqrt{(x-c)^2 + y^2} = 2a.$$

It looks messy, but it’s just algebra: move one square root over, square both sides, simplify, isolate the other square root, and square again. You end up with

$$\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{a^2 - c^2} = 1,$$

and since we just showed $a^2 - c^2 = b^2$, that’s the standard form:

$$\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1.$$

Quick check: if you set $a = b$ (so $c = 0$ and both foci land on the center), this turns into $x^2 + y^2 = a^2$—just a circle of radius $a$. So a circle really is an ellipse with $e = 0$, which is exactly why we’re allowed to use a circle as the easy case when we derive Kepler’s 3rd Law.

Kepler’s Second Law: Derivation

Abir Mehta

What is Kepler’s 2nd Law?

Kepler’s 2nd Law states that an imaginary line joining a planet to the Sun sweeps out equal areas in equal times. Refer to Fig. 1 for a visual representation of the law.

At first, this seems so abstract. How would you even measure areas swept out, especially on an ellipse—such a weird shape? Well think about it like this: in a time $\Delta t$, the planet should sweep out an area $\Delta A$ relative to its Sun that sits at one of the foci. If Kepler’s 2nd Law holds, then the quantity $\Delta A /\Delta t$ should remain constant, no matter how small or large the time interval $\Delta t$ is. And $\Delta A /\Delta t$ is really just the change in area over the change in time—which is exactly what differentiation measures! So we can write it as a derivative,

$$\frac{dA}{dt} \;\; \text{should remain constant!}$$

Now, we’ve reduces Kepler’s 2nd Law from an unintuitive representation to something we can mathematically work with! Sounds pretty cool!

Understanding Kepler’s 2nd Law is only the first step; we need to go deeper into the background information!

Background Information

Newton’s Universal Law of Gravitation: Vector Form

I’m sure you guys have heard of Newton’s Universal Law of Gravitation, but only represented in magnitudes:

$$F_g = \frac{GMm}{r^2}.$$

Pretty familiar right? Well here is the vector form:

$$\vec{F}_g = -\frac{GMm}{r^2} \hat{r}.$$

The vector form of Newton’s universal law of gravitation simply gives both the magnitude and direction of the force of gravity acting on mass $m$ due to mass $M$. Here, $r$ is the distance between the two masses measured along the line connecting them. We assume that the mass $M$ is fixed in place for simplicity (though this is not exactly true if you want to do some further digging).

Okay, there might be a few things going through your mind: what is that little hat doing on the $r$? what do you mean by a “line connecting them”? where does the negative sign pop up from? If the hat notation is new to you, the Vectors & Ellipses tab has you covered—here we’ll just look at what it means for gravity. Let’s break it down with an illustration!

The symbol $\hat{r}$ is a unit vector pointing along the line connecting $M$ and $m$. Key distinction: it points from $M$ to to $m$. Its direction continuously changes as $m$ moves relative to $M$.

In the context of classical mechanics (as you may have been taught), gravity is a central force that’s also an “action-at-a-distance”. As shown in Fig.~2, we place our central body $M$ at the origin, so $\hat{r}$ points away from $M$. But the force of gravity on $m$ pulls it toward $M$—the opposite direction. That’s where the negative sign comes from! Our vector form

$$\vec{F}_g = -\frac{GMm}{r^2} \hat{r}$$

takes a step in the right direction ;) which is good! Well, what about the magnitude? Well because $\hat{r}$ is a unit vector, the magnitude of $\vec{F}_g$ is again $GMm/r^2$. This aligns exactly with the ‘magnitude-only’ form you were taught earlier.

Make you understand Newton’s Universal Law of Gravitation in its vector form, because it is vital first step for the derivation.

The other tool we’ll need is the vector cross product, since that’s how torque is defined. I’ve put the full explanation over on the Vectors & Ellipses tab to keep this page focused—give it a look if you need a refresher.

Deriving Kepler’s 2nd Law

The Setup

Suppose we have a large mass $M$ fixed at the origin of our coordinate system. Compared to $m$, its tiny revolutions about the system’s center of mass are negligible—so we treat it as stationary. The mass $m$ follows an elliptical orbit about $M$, which sits at one of the foci.

We can describe the position of $m$ at any moment with a position vector $\vec{r}(t)$, which gives both the direction and distance of $m$ from the origin. Since $\vec{r}(t)$ points along our familiar unit vector $\hat{r}$ and has magnitude $r$ (the distance between $M$ and $m$), we can write $\vec{r}(t) = r\hat{r}$.

In terms of cross products, the vector form of torque $\vec{\tau}$ is defined as

$$\vec{\tau} = \vec{r} \times \vec{F}.$$

Notice how this aligns with the typical formula for torque that deals with magnitudes:

$$\tau = rF \sin \theta.$$

Refer back to the cross product section (on the Vectors & Ellipses tab) if you are confused.

Kepler’s Third Law: Derivation

Abir Mehta

What is Kepler’s 3rd Law?

Okay, last one! Kepler’s 3rd Law states that the square of a planet’s orbital period $T$ is proportional to the cube of its semi-major axis $a$:

$$T^2 \propto a^3.$$

Refer to Fig. 1 for a visual representation of the law.

If the term “semi-major axis” is unfamiliar, no worries—you can find a full breakdown of what $a$ means over on the Vectors & Ellipses tab. For a circular orbit, though, it’s nothing complicated: $a$ is just the radius!

So what does this law actually tell us? The farther a planet is from the Sun, the longer its orbital period—that part you could probably guess. But the period doesn’t grow proportionally with distance. Push a planet out to twice the orbital size and its period doesn’t just double, it stretches by a factor of $2\sqrt{2} \approx 2.83$! Kepler uncovered this pattern by poring over Brahe’s data, and now we get to derive it ourselves from Newton’s mechanics. Pretty cool, right?

We’ll derive it for the simplest case: a perfectly circular orbit, where the semi-major axis $a$ is just the radius $r$. The same result holds for elliptical orbits too, but proving that needs the full orbit equation, which is a bit beyond our scope here.

Deriving Kepler’s 3rd Law

The Setup

Our setup is similar to the 2nd Law: a large mass $M$ fixed at the origin, with a small mass $m$ orbiting around it. The only difference this time is that $m$ moves along a circular orbit of radius $r$.

Gravity is still the only force acting on $m$, and we know its magnitude:

$$F_g = \frac{GMm}{r^2}.$$

Now, for a circular orbit, gravity always points directly toward the center—and that’s exactly the direction you need to keep something moving in a circle! In other words, gravity supplies the centripetal force that keeps the planet in its orbit. That’s the fact we’ll build the derivation on.

Result

You should arrive at

$$\boxed{T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{GM} r^3.}$$

And there it is—Kepler’s 3rd Law, derived straight from Newton’s law of gravitation! Notice the constant out front, $4\pi^2/(GM)$: it depends only on the central mass $M$, not on the orbiting planet. This means every planet orbiting the same Sun shares the same value of $T^2/r^3$. Kepler spotted this pattern just by studying Brahe’s data; Newton’s mechanics is what finally explains why it’s true.

Does this actually hold for ellipses?

We pulled this off for a circle, where $a = r$. But real planets move on ellipses—so does the law survive? It does! All you do is trade the radius $r$ for the semi-major axis $a$, and it reads

$$T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{GM} a^3,$$

same constant out front, same $T^2 \propto a^3$. The honest catch is that nailing this down for a general ellipse takes the full orbit equation, which is more than we want to get into here. If you want to see the elliptical case worked all the way through, Taylor’s Classical Mechanics (§8.5–8.6) is a great place to look.
