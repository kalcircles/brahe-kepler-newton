Kepler's Second Law: Derivation

What is Kepler's 2nd Law?

Kepler's 2nd Law states that an imaginary line joining a planet to the Sun sweeps out equal areas in equal times. Refer to Fig. 1 for a visual representation of the law.

At first, this seems so abstract. How would you even measure areas swept out, especially on an ellipse—such a weird shape? Well think about it like this: in a time $\Delta t$, the planet should sweep out an area $\Delta A$ relative to its Sun that sits at one of the foci. If Kepler's 2nd Law holds, then the quantity $\Delta A /\Delta t$ should remain constant, no matter how small or large the time interval $\Delta t$ is. We can take this to the differential limit; in other words

$$\frac{dA}{dt} \;\; \text{should remain constant!}$$

Now, we've reduces Kepler's 2nd Law from an unintuitive representation to something we can mathematically work with! Sounds pretty cool!

Understanding Kepler's 2nd Law is only the first step; we need to go deeper into the background information!

Background Information

Newton's Universal Law of Gravitation: Vector Form

I'm sure you guys have heard of Newton's Universal Law of Gravitation, but only represented in magnitudes:

$$F_g = \frac{GMm}{r^2}.$$

Pretty familiar right? Well here is the vector form:

$$\vec{F}_g = -\frac{GMm}{r^2}\, \hat{r}.$$

The vector form of Newton's universal law of gravitation simply gives both the magnitude and direction of the force of gravity acting on mass $m$ due to mass $M$. Here, $r$ is the distance between the two masses measured along the line connecting them. We assume that the mass $M$ is fixed in place for simplicity (though this is not exactly true if you want to do some further digging).

Okay, there might be a few things going through your mind: what is the extra $r$ doing with a little hat symbol that we call the circonflexe? what do you mean by a “line connecting them”? where does the negative sign pop up from? Let's break it down with an illustration!

The symbol $\hat{r}$ is a unit vector pointing along the line connecting $M$ and $m$. Key distinction: it points from M to to m. Because it is a unit vector, its magnitude is always $1$ (hence the name). However, its direction continuously changes as $m$ moves relative to $M$.

In the context of classical mechanics (as you may have been taught), gravity is a central force that's also an “action-at-a-distance”. As shown in Fig. 2, we place our central body $M$ at the origin, so $\hat{r}$ points away from $M$. But the force of gravity on $m$ pulls it toward $M$—the opposite direction. That's where the negative sign comes from! Our vector form

$$\vec{F}_g = -\frac{GMm}{r^2}\, \hat{r}$$

takes a step in the right direction ;) which is good! Well, what about the magnitude? Well because $\hat{r}$ is a unit vector, the magnitude of $\vec{F}_g$ is again $GMm/r^2$. This aligns exactly with the ‘magnitude-only’ form you were taught earlier.

Make you understand Newton's Universal Law of Gravitation in its vector form, because it is vital first step for the derivation.

The Vector Cross Product

The vector cross product seems like a tricky concept, but understanding its physical representation is more fundamental to your understanding of it, despite its

Suppose, we have two vectors $\vec{A}$ and $\vec{B}$. Their cross product $\vec{A} \times \vec{B}$ is another vector $\vec{C}$. $\vec{C}$ has magnitude $|\vec{A}||\vec{B}|\sin \theta_{AB}$ where $\theta_{AB}$ is the angle between vector $\vec{A}$ and $\vec{B}$. More importantly, however, $\vec{C}$ is mutually perpendicular (orthogonal) to both $\vec{A}$ and $\vec{B}$. Not sure what that looks like? Check out the figure below!

Note that if $\vec{A}$ and $\vec{B}$ are parallel or anti-parallel, then $\theta_{AB} = 0$ or $\pi$, so $|\vec{C}| = |\vec{A}||\vec{B}|\sin \theta_{AB} = 0$. In other words, the cross product of two aligned vectors is always zero.

That's basically all you need to know! The rest of the information will be specific to the setup of the problem, and accessible from your AP Physics knowledge.

Deriving Kepler's 2nd Law

The Setup

Suppose we have a large mass $M$ fixed at the origin of our coordinate system. Compared to $m$, its tiny revolutions about the system's center of mass are negligible—so we treat it as stationary. The mass $m$ follows an elliptical orbit about $M$, which sits at one of the foci.

We can describe the position of $m$ at any moment with a position vector $\vec{r}(t)$, which gives both the direction and distance of $m$ from the origin. Since $\vec{r}(t)$ points along our familiar unit vector $\hat{r}$ and has magnitude $r$ (the distance between $M$ and $m$), we can write $\vec{r}(t) = r\hat{r}$.

In terms of cross products, the vector form of torque $\vec{\tau}$ is defined as

$$\vec{\tau} = \vec{r} \times \vec{F}.$$

Notice how this aligns with the typical formula for torque that deals with magnitudes:

$$\tau = rF \sin \theta.$$

Refer back to the section on the vector cross product if you are confused.

Kepler's Third Law: Derivation

What is Kepler's 3rd Law?

Kepler's 3rd Law states that the square of a planet's orbital period is proportional to the cube of its semi-major axis. In other words, if $T$ is the time it takes a planet to complete one full orbit and $a$ is the semi-major axis of the ellipse, then

$$T^2 \propto a^3.$$

Refer to Fig. 5 for a visual representation of the law.

What does this actually mean? Think of it this way: a planet farther from the Sun has a longer year, and not just proportionally longer—it grows faster than you'd expect. Double the orbital size and the period increases by a factor of $2\sqrt{2} \approx 2.83$. Kepler's 3rd Law captures that precise relationship.

We will derive this law for the special case of a circular orbit, where the semi-major axis $a$ reduces to the orbital radius $r$. The result generalizes to ellipses, but that proof requires the full orbit equation—which is beyond the scope of our discussion.

Deriving Kepler's 3rd Law

The Setup

Same setup as our Kepler's 2nd Law derivation: a large mass $M$ sits at the origin, and a smaller mass $m$ orbits around it. This time, however, we restrict ourselves to a circular orbit of radius $r$.

The gravitational force on $m$ due to $M$ is the only force acting on the planet. From our earlier discussion, its magnitude is

$$F_g = \frac{GMm}{r^2}.$$

This force points radially inward—exactly the direction needed for centripetal acceleration. That's no coincidence!

Step 1 · Equating Forces

Since gravity is the only force and it points toward the center, it must provide the centripetal force. Using what you know about uniform circular motion, set the gravitational force equal to the centripetal force. What equation do you get?

Hint 1

What is the centripetal force required to keep a mass $m$ moving in a circle of radius $r$ at speed $v$?

Hint 2

You should have

$$\frac{GMm}{r^2} = \frac{mv^2}{r}.$$
Hint 3

Notice that $m$ cancels from both sides! This means the orbital motion doesn't depend on the planet's mass—only on $M$. Pretty cool, right? Simplify by cancelling $m$ and multiplying both sides by $r$.

Answer

After simplification:

$$\frac{GM}{r} = v^2.$$
Step 2 · Eliminate the Speed

You have an expression relating $v$, $r$, $G$, and $M$. But Kepler's 3rd Law involves the orbital period $T$, not the speed $v$. Think about what a circular orbit looks like geometrically: in one period $T$, the planet traces out the full circle. Can you express $v$ in terms of $r$ and $T$?

0 / 3
Hint 1

What is the total distance the planet travels in one orbit? What's the time it takes?

Hint 2

The circumference of the orbit is $2\pi r$, and it takes time $T$ to complete. So $v = 2\pi r / T$. Substitute this into your result from Step 1.

Answer

You should have

$$\frac{GM}{r} = \frac{4\pi^2 r^2}{T^2}.$$
Step 3 · Solve for $T^2$

You're almost there! Rearrange the expression from Step 2 to isolate $T^2$ on one side. What does the result tell you about the relationship between $T$ and $r$? No hints for this one!

Result

You should arrive at

$$T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{GM}\, r^3.$$

That's Kepler's 3rd Law for circular orbits! The factor $4\pi^2/(GM)$ is a constant for any planet orbiting the same central mass $M$, which means $T^2/r^3$ is the same for every planet in the solar system. Kepler discovered this empirically from Brahe's data—and Newton's theory of gravity explains why.

A Note on Elliptical Orbits

We derived this for a circular orbit where the radius $r$ is constant. For an elliptical orbit, the result generalizes to

$$T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{GM}\, a^3,$$

where $a$ is the semi-major axis of the ellipse. Proving this requires solving the full equation of motion under gravity to obtain the elliptical orbit equation—a derivation beyond the scope of this activity. But the physical intuition is the same: gravity provides the force that sustains the orbit, and the geometry does the rest.
