Introduction

In this problem you will work with the remarkably precise planetary position data set recorded by Danish astronomer Tycho Brahe without a telescope from 1582 to 1600 CE to understand the relationship between Earth-based observations of planetary positions, their heliocentric orbits, Kepler’s empirically derived Laws of Planetary Motion, and Newton’s explanatory Law of Universal Gravitation.

Part 1

Background

Kepler’s Laws demonstrated that planets follow elliptical orbits with the Sun at one focus, and Newton’s Law of Universal Gravitation provided a theoretical framework for why these orbits must be ellipses.

Before we get there, however, we are going to start by understanding the mathematical conversion between the declination of Mars as observed from Brahe’s observatory on the island of Hven, 55.90778° N latitude and 12.69667° E longitude, and the position vector of Mars relative to the Earth as viewed in the “universe frame” in which we are looking at the orbits of Mars and Earth around the Sun.

Start with the following assumptions; we will iteratively improve the model over the course of this problem.

  • Both Earth and Mars are in circular orbits about the Sun, which is at the center of each orbit.
  • The orbits of Earth and Mars are co-planar.
  • Both Earth and Mars are orbiting the Sun at constant speed.

As our diagram shows, you can travel to Mars directly from the Sun by following $\mathbf{r_{MS}}$!

With this in mind, can you write an equation that expresses this relationship?

The equation that represents this relationship is

$$\mathbf{r_{ES}} + \mathbf{r_{ME}} = \mathbf{r_{MS}}.$$
Key Idea

Vectors can be added head-to-tail to represent a sequence of motion or displacement.

Suppose a planet is moving around a circle of radius $r$, and its angular position is $\theta$. If the Sun is at the origin, how can you express the planet’s $x$- and $y$-coordinates in terms of $r$ and $\theta$?

Hint 2≈48%

Recall that in a right triangle,

$$\cos\theta = \frac{x}{r}, \qquad \sin\theta = \frac{y}{r}.$$

How can we write $x$ and $y$ in terms of $\cos\theta$, $\sin\theta$, and $r$? Rewriting the above-mentioned equations leads us to

$$x = r\cos\theta \qquad \text{and} \qquad y = r\sin\theta.$$
Hint 3≈72%

Consider Mars. Its angular speed is $\omega_M$, so after time $t$, what is its angular position $\theta_M$?

Use the same idea for Earth. Its angular speed is $\omega_E$, so what is its angular position $\theta_E$?

Answer100%

Once you have the coordinates of Earth and Mars, note that rearranging the result from Problem 2, we have

$$\mathbf{r_{ME}} = \mathbf{r_{MS}} - \mathbf{r_{ES}}.$$

Can you use this to find the components of $\mathbf{r_{ME}}$?

If a point is moving on a circle of radius $r$ with angular position $\theta$, its Cartesian coordinates are

$$x = r\cos\theta, \qquad y = r\sin\theta.$$

Since both planets begin on the $x$-axis and move with constant angular speed, their angular positions after time $t$ are

$$\theta_M = \omega_M t$$

for Mars, and

$$\theta_E = \omega_E t$$

for Earth.

Therefore, the position vector of Mars relative to the Sun is

$$\mathbf{r_{MS}}(t) = \langle r_M\cos(\omega_M t),\, r_M\sin(\omega_M t)\rangle$$

and the position vector of Earth relative to the Sun is

$$\mathbf{r_{ES}}(t) = \langle r_E\cos(\omega_E t),\, r_E\sin(\omega_E t)\rangle.$$

Problem 2 tells us that

$$\mathbf{r_{ES}} + \mathbf{r_{ME}} = \mathbf{r_{MS}},$$

which can be rewritten as

$$\mathbf{r_{ME}} = \mathbf{r_{MS}} - \mathbf{r_{ES}}.$$

Substituting our expressions yields

$$\mathbf{r_{ME}}(t) = \langle r_M\cos(\omega_M t),\, r_M\sin(\omega_M t)\rangle - \langle r_E\cos(\omega_E t),\, r_E\sin(\omega_E t)\rangle,$$

and subtracting the components gives

$$\mathbf{r_{ME}}(t) = \langle r_M\cos(\omega_M t) - r_E\cos(\omega_E t),\; r_M\sin(\omega_M t) - r_E\sin(\omega_E t)\rangle.$$

Thus, we’ve written the components of all three vectors in terms of the given values.
